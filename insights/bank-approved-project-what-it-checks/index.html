"Bank-approved project": what a bank's approval really checks

Arvind Patidar
Advisory · Execution • Published: 19 Oct 2026 • 13 min read
Share:

Short answer: A bank approved project is one a lender has pre-checked so it can process home loans faster. It usually covers title, building plan approvals and the builder's papers at one point in time. It isn't a quality certificate, a delivery promise or a promise that you'll get the loan. Some banks say so on their own lists.

Key takeaways
  • It's a processing shortcut. One bank reviews the project once, so each buyer's property check goes faster.
  • Banks disclaim it themselves. Canara Bank's list says approval doesn't certify the builder, title, approvals, quality or delivery.
  • It's a snapshot. Approval reflects documents on the date checked, for the towers or phases checked.
  • RBI rules help you: loans for under-construction flats should be released by construction stage, and builders with bank project loans must disclose the mortgage.
  • Your own checks still matter: RERA filings, the encumbrance certificate, the mortgage NOC and the OC.
  • Our SCOPE check: snapshot date, coverage, ownership of risk, project mortgage, escrow and RERA.

You'll see the line on hoardings and brochures across Bengaluru: "approved by leading banks". It sounds like someone careful has signed off on the project. Someone has, but for the bank's purposes, not yours. This piece sets out what lenders actually check, what they say they don't, and how to use the label without leaning on it.

What does "bank-approved" actually mean?

A builder asks a bank to review a project. The bank looks at the land title, the approved plans and the clearances, and if it's satisfied, it lists the project and gives it a reference number. Lenders call this an APF number. ICICI Home Finance describes the APF number as a code given after the lender reviews "key legal, technical, and approval-related documents" (ICICI HFC). Kotak describes it as approved project finance (Kotak).

The purpose is efficiency. Canara Bank's list says approval is "mainly for the purpose of eliminating possible delay by avoiding repetitive jobs in processing of loan applications from different purchasers" in the same project (Canara Bank).

What do banks say their approval doesn't mean?

Canara Bank's page is unusually direct. It says that listing a project:

  • "does not mean that the project is being recommended by the Bank";
  • shouldn't be read as the bank vouching for "the competence of the Builder, facilities, title of the property, regulatory approvals, quality of construction or delivery schedules";
  • doesn't give buyers a right to a loan; sanction stays at the bank's discretion.

It also says approval is "project-specific and not builder-specific", and that the bank won't be a party to disputes between buyer and builder (Canara Bank). ICICI HFC similarly says APF approval doesn't assure you of home loan approval or of the project's completion (ICICI HFC).

That's the bank's own position. Read the brochure line in that light.

What do lenders typically check?

From lender pages, the usual project checks are below. Each bank sets its own list, and it isn't published in full, so treat this as typical rather than standard.

Check What the lender looks at What it still leaves to you
Land title Title documents and a legal report Your own EC on Kaveri and a lawyer's title view for your unit
Building plan Sanctioned plan from the planning authority Whether your tower and floor match the sanction
Clearances Approvals needed for the project Conditions in those approvals, like setbacks or a pending OC
RERA Registration details, where applicable Quarterly updates, delay history, complaints
Builder Background and financial standing Delivery record on other projects
Construction stage Progress for under-construction towers Whether progress matches the payment plan you signed

What do RBI rules require of banks?

Three parts of RBI's Master Circular on Housing Finance matter to buyers (RBI):

  • Approvals before money. Banks should ensure the borrower has prior permission from the authorities, where required. Loans can be sanctioned normally, but "disbursements should be made only after" clearances are obtained (para 6).
  • Stage-linked release. Housing loans to individuals "should be closely linked to the stages of construction", and "upfront disbursal should not be made" for incomplete or under-construction projects (para 4). RBI names the risks of 80:20 and similar schemes, including the builder failing to pay EMIs it promised to cover.
  • Mortgage disclosure. When a bank funds a housing project, the builder must name the mortgagee bank in brochures and ads, and state that it will provide the bank's NOC for selling flats if required. Banks shouldn't release funds until the builder complies (para 7).

So a project brochure should tell you which bank holds a charge over the project, and the builder should be able to produce that bank's NOC for your flat.

How does RERA fit with a bank approval?

RERA gives you rights that don't depend on any bank. When registering, the promoter files a sworn declaration on legal title and that "the land is free from all encumbrances", or gives details of them, plus the completion timeline and the 70% separate-account rule (RERA Act, s.4(2)(l)). After signing an agreement for sale, the promoter must not mortgage your flat, and any such mortgage "shall not affect the right and interest of the allottee" (s.11(4)(h)).

Those declarations are on the K-RERA portal for every registered project. Learn to read them with our step-by-step RERA check, and see how we review filings in our RERA project review.

Worked example 1: what stage-linked release means for your pocket

You buy a ₹1 crore under-construction flat with a ₹75 lakh loan at an illustrative 8.5%. The project is at 30% and the bank has released ₹30 lakh.

Step Amount
Released so far ₹30,00,000
Monthly pre-EMI interest (₹30 lakh × 8.5% ÷ 12) ₹21,250
If the project stalls at this stage for 12 months ₹2,55,000 in interest
Full EMI once ₹75 lakh is out (₹867.82 per lakh, 20 years) ₹65,087

Stage-linked release keeps your interest bill tied to real progress. Compare a scheme where the full ₹75 lakh goes to the builder upfront and the builder promises to pay the interest. If it stops paying for six months, ₹75 lakh × 8.5% ÷ 12 is ₹53,125 a month, or ₹3,18,750 overdue, and that shows up on your credit record, not the builder's. RBI flags exactly this risk. Ask how any "no EMI till possession" offer is structured before you sign (20 questions before signing a payment plan).

Worked example 2: the cost of switching to the "approved" bank

The builder says only one bank has approved the project, and its rate is 0.25 points above your own bank's offer. Illustrative figures on a ₹75 lakh, 20-year loan:

Your bank, 8.5% Approved bank, 8.75%
EMI ₹65,087 ₹66,278
Extra per month ₹1,192
Extra over 240 months ₹2,85,975

Faster processing can be worth something, but you can also ask your own bank to review the project. ICICI Bank's blog notes that approval by one bank doesn't mean another will approve it, and that you can ask your bank to take up the review, though it takes time (ICICI Bank). If your bank declines, ask why. That answer can tell you more than any approval list (home loan questions to ask).

The SCOPE check (our framework)

Before you treat "bank-approved" as a reason to book, run SCOPE:

  • S: Snapshot date. When did the bank review the project? Ask for the approval letter's date or check the bank's current list.
  • C: Coverage. Which towers, phases or units does the approval cover? Phase 2 may not have been reviewed.
  • O: Ownership of risk. The bank's check protects its security. Your loss if the project slips isn't its concern.
  • P: Project mortgage. Does a bank hold a charge over the land or project? Get the mortgagee's NOC or release letter for your flat before registration.
  • E: Escrow and RERA. Read the RERA title declaration, the encumbrance details and the quarterly progress filings yourself.

Checklist before relying on a bank approval

  • [ ] Project appears on the bank's own current list, not just the brochure
  • [ ] Approval covers your tower or phase
  • [ ] Brochure names any bank holding a mortgage over the project
  • [ ] Mortgagee NOC or release letter for your flat promised in writing
  • [ ] RERA registration, title declaration and encumbrance details read on K-RERA
  • [ ] Encumbrance certificate pulled on Kaveri
  • [ ] Building plan sanction and approving authority checked
  • [ ] Loan release stage-linked; no upfront disbursal for an under-construction flat
  • [ ] Any EMI-holiday or subvention offer read in the tripartite agreement
  • [ ] Your own lawyer's title opinion on the unit

What mistakes do buyers make?

  • Reading approval as a recommendation. Banks say it isn't.
  • Skipping their own legal check because "the bank already did it".
  • Ignoring the project mortgage. Registering without the lender's NOC for your flat.
  • Signing subvention deals blind. Not reading who pays interest and what happens if they stop.
  • Assuming all phases are covered when the approval was for an earlier phase.
  • Switching to a pricier loan only because it's on the brochure.

What should you do if...

...your own bank won't approve the project? Ask for the reason in writing. A title or approval gap is a reason to pause, not to switch banks.

...the brochure doesn't name any mortgagee bank? Ask the builder directly whether the land or project is mortgaged, and check the RERA encumbrance declaration and the EC.

...the builder offers "no EMI till possession"? Ask for the tripartite agreement, check whether money is released upfront, and work out what you'd owe if the builder stops paying.

...the project is behind schedule? Check the quarterly RERA updates and know your options under RERA s.18.

...you're buying in a panchayat area? Lender appetite and paperwork differ; see our answer on loans for Hoskote panchayat flats.

...possession is offered without an OC? Read OC and CC explained before you take keys.
